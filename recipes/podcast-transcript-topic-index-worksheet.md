# Build a browsable topic index for an existing podcast transcript

By TubeText Labs · Prepared 4 November 2026

You have an episode transcript, but searching for one keyword still leaves you guessing where a question starts and where the answer ends. Make a small topic index alongside the transcript. Each entry should help a reader find a specific passage, even when the text has no timestamps.

This worksheet is for manual reading and navigation. It takes an existing transcript as input; it does not fetch audio, run transcription, generate player chapters or establish that the words are accurate.

## Start with a stable source

Use a publisher-provided transcript when available and permitted. If you already have a transcript from a TubeText product, use the actual text in your successful result. You do not need another transcription run to organize text you already hold.

Keep the original unchanged. Save an episode URL, transcript URL or filename, and the date you obtained that version. A transcript from an ad-free edition may have different timing from a public recording, so identify the edition too. Record “unknown” when you cannot establish it.

Create the index as a separate note. If you want a tidier reading copy first, use the [reading-copy guide](transcript-reading-copy-without-losing-source.md), retaining the source it came from.

## Decide what counts as one entry

Skim the transcript once for changes in the question being discussed. Start an entry when the conversation moves to a different useful question, rather than after a fixed number of minutes or words.

- Use a short, neutral label a reader could recognize: “Finding a saved download,” rather than “Great tips.”
- Separate two answers if a listener would reasonably look for them independently.
- Keep a digression separate when it interrupts an answer. If the original topic resumes later, give that later passage its own locator under the same topic.
- Add one or two alternate search words when the transcript uses unfamiliar wording. Label these as your index terms, not words the speaker necessarily said.
- Leave an unclear section unclassified until you can review it. Do not turn a guessed subject into a confident heading.

The goal is a compact map of the episode, not a replacement transcript or a claim-by-claim research summary.

## Choose a locator that the source actually supports

For plain text, use a short exact phrase near the beginning and another near the end of the passage. Try Find in the preserved source to check whether each phrase is unique. If a phrase repeats, lengthen it or record which occurrence and the nearby heading.

Paragraph numbers can help in your own saved copy if you number it consistently, but they may change when the publisher edits or reflows the transcript. Record the filename or version they refer to. Do not imply those numbers are stable links on the publisher's website.

If the source already contains timestamps, you may record them separately with the matching media edition. Only call a time checked after comparing it with that recording. Leave time blank when none is supplied; do not estimate it from word count, paragraph position or a different edition.

An index entry is a text locator. It does not create a clickable player jump, a chapter file, synchronized highlighting or word-level timing. See the [reading versus highlighting FAQ](podcast-transcript-reading-vs-highlighting.md) for those distinctions.

## Fictional text-only example

The following three lines are invented to demonstrate the worksheet. They are not a real episode, customer result or Actor output.

**Saved source: fictional-episode.txt**

1. “Where did the downloaded episode go? Open your saved downloads list and look for the episode title.”
2. “What about storage space? Check the app's storage view before removing anything you want to keep.”
3. “Back to finding that episode: the title can differ from the filename you see in a file browser.”

An index could contain these entries:

| Topic | Start phrase | End phrase | Location in this fictional copy | Related passage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Finding a downloaded episode | Where did the downloaded episode go? | look for the episode title. | Line 1 | Line 3 resumes the topic |
| Checking storage before removal | What about storage space? | anything you want to keep. | Line 2 | None identified |
| Episode title versus filename | Back to finding that episode: | in a file browser. | Line 3 | Line 1 |

The example has no timestamp column filled in because it has no timed source. Its labels and cross-references are manual additions, not transcript product features. No player link is implied.

## Test whether another reader can use it

Before sharing, take a topic from the index and locate its full passage using only the supplied source identity and locators. Check that:

- Both boundary phrases exist in that exact source version.
- A repeated phrase cannot send the reader to the wrong passage.
- The boundaries include the question and any qualification needed to understand the answer.
- A resumed topic points to the later passage rather than implying the whole answer is contiguous.
- Missing or unverified timing stays clearly marked.
- Any short excerpts and the source you share are permitted to be shared. An index does not grant permission to republish someone else's transcript.

If you need to cite a statement as evidence, return to the original transcript and recording and verify it. Browsing labels are navigation aids, not verified quotations or speaker attribution.

## Why this worksheet exists

A [January 2022 public listener discussion](https://www.twit.community/t/where-is-the-link-to-the-questions-and-answers-written-out-for-the-shows/11462) described difficulty finding individual questions inside long transcripts. It is historical context for this reader task, not a current support request, partnership or evidence of TubeText adoption.

TubeText Labs maintains the linked guides. This AI-assisted worksheet describes a manual workflow over existing text, with a wholly fictional example. It has not been presented as a tested transcription run, automatic chapter feature or measured improvement in reading speed.
