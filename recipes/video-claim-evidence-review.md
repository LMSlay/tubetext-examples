# Review each video claim before summarizing the whole clip

By TubeText Labs · Static teaching example · 3 November 2026

A reviewer can have enough evidence to describe one moment and still be unable to assess another claim in the same video. This exercise shows how to hand over those different decisions without turning a readable AI summary into a verdict on the entire clip.

**The video, notes, times and answers below are invented.** No media was inspected, and no Actor execution, model evaluation or product test was performed. Treat the supplied observations as stipulated facts of a paper exercise. In real work, check them against the permitted source first. These worksheet labels are not product output fields.

## Start with a fixed review list

Imagine a 12-second demonstration of a desk cable clip. Before reviewing it, write down these four questions:

1. Does the supplied speech excerpt say that the clip holds four cables?
2. Does the supplied visual note describe four visible cable segments inside one clip?
3. Does the evidence establish that the clip cuts setup time in half?
4. Does the final screen direct viewers to setup instructions?

Keep all four questions in the handoff, including work you have not started. This is a chosen review list, not a claim that every possible statement in a video has been identified.

## Read the fictional evidence packet

Reference **D1** is invented; it has no real URL, creator, publication date or run ID.

| Note | Supplied material |
|---|---|
| S1, speech at 00:01–00:03 | “This clip holds four cables together.” |
| V1, visual note at 00:04–00:06 | Four visible cable segments sit inside one clip on a desk. No load or durability test is described. |
| T1, screen text at 00:06–00:08 | “Cut your setup time in half.” |
| Review coverage | The reviewer has considered S1, V1 and T1. The final screen at 00:10–00:12 has not been inspected or described. No timed comparison is supplied. |

An invented draft summary says: **“The demonstration proves the clip halves setup time, and the video gives complete setup instructions.”** This sentence is written as a teaching error, not an observed AI response.

## Make a separate decision for each question

Record **evidence status** before the **finding**. “Sufficient” means sufficient for the exact question being asked, not true, trustworthy or complete in every respect.

**1. The wording of the speech**

- Evidence status: **sufficient within this exercise**, using S1.
- Finding: S1 contains the statement that the clip holds four cables together.
- Boundary: this establishes what the supplied excerpt says. It does not independently establish the clip's capability, accurate transcription or the speaker's identity.

**2. The visible arrangement**

- Evidence status: **sufficient within this exercise**, using V1.
- Finding: the supplied note describes four visible cable segments inside one clip during that interval.
- Boundary: do not expand that observation into a claim about all cable sizes, durability or performance over time. In real work, verify the interval before using the note as an observation.

**3. The claimed time saving**

- Evidence status: **insufficient**, after reviewing S1, V1 and T1.
- Finding: **withhold the performance conclusion**. T1 states it, but the packet provides no defined baseline, comparable timed tasks or timing results.
- Next check: identify what “setup” includes and what comparison would support the claim. The worksheet does not authorize or conduct that work.
- Boundary: insufficient support does not establish that the claim is false. Do not relabel it “refuted.”

**4. The final screen**

- Evidence status: **not yet assessed**. The reviewer has not reviewed that part of the source.
- Finding: **none recorded**. Do not fill the cell with “no instructions,” “insufficient” or “false” merely to finish the table.
- Next check: inspect the permitted final interval and record what it contains. Even a link labeled “instructions” would not establish that the linked instructions are complete or correct.

## Hand over the unfinished work too

The review list contains **four questions**. Three have an evidence-sufficiency decision: two sufficient for narrow observations and one insufficient for a performance conclusion. One question remains unreviewed.

If reporting the share without a substantive finding, include both the withheld performance conclusion and the unreviewed question: **2 of 4**, with the two reasons shown separately. This is worksheet coverage, not accuracy, credibility or a measured product result. Do not summarize it as “the video is 50% true.”

A suitable handoff would say:

> Within the fictional packet, S1 states that the clip holds four cables, and V1 describes four cable segments in one clip. The time-saving claim is unsupported by the supplied comparison evidence. The final screen has not been reviewed. No whole-video truth or performance conclusion is available.

## Use this beside an existing creative review

Retain the original material and keep generated descriptions, checked observations, claim-specific decisions and unresolved work separate. For a real handoff, record the permitted source, version, exact interval, reviewer and review date. Preserve the reasons for withheld findings rather than dropping those rows from a clean-looking summary.
